SINGLE SURV

A backstage dispatch from Berlin Fashion Week — on candid photography, borrowed access, and the models who only exist behind the flash.
Vol. 03Editorial9 min read
Written by [Author Name]Photography Generated with RealSnap

"Who are you with?" "Single Surv." A short answer is a hard one to check. Outside a show venue during Berlin Fashion Week, that's the whole of what I'd prepared to say.

No agency, no name, no host to point to — none of it was needed. Wear black, carry a camera, walk fast, and most people don't push the question further. Before a show starts, everyone is carrying a problem more urgent than mine. Still, just in case, I'd tucked a pass bought secondhand into my jacket. The service door at the back of the building was propped half open. I slipped in with a knot of crew returning from a smoke break.

Black cable lay tangled across the floor. Unopened water and a rack of hangers were stacked along one side of the hallway. Old brick and peeling plaster, dust-caked curtains, temporary lighting rigged above them. The music came through the wall less as rhythm than as vibration. Instead of showing a pass, I held the camera up against my chest. A camera is sometimes more convincing than an ID.

What is backstage, exactly? Literally, the space behind the stage — but at a fashion show it's neither fully private nor fully public. While models change, touch up makeup, and wait their turn, the house photographer and the brand's content team are recording all of it without pause. Backstage photography is one more image in the show's construction: arranged to look less arranged than the runway, chosen to look accidental.

So what separates a photo taken secretly from a photo that only looks that way? Is it whether the subject knew the camera was there? Whether the photographer had permission? Or does a picture become private simply by the visual evidence of it — a focus slightly missed, a frame cropped wrong, a flash that overexposes skin?

My camera rarely came up to eye level. I kept it near my waist and pressed the shutter, and a good part of every frame held something I hadn't meant to look at. A shoulder blocked the view; instead of a model's face, the shoes kicked off under a chair came in sharp. A hard flash lifted one person out of the dark and buried everyone behind them back into it. The more evidence there was of a shot gone wrong, the more, oddly, it read as something to believe.

The German photographer Erich Salomon spent the late 1920s photographing politicians and public figures with a small Ermanox camera hidden in a hat or a bag. The people he caught weren't facing the lens the way they did in official portraits. They yawned mid-meeting, traded jokes with the person next to them, slumped in their chairs. In 1929, the phrase "candid camera" entered use to describe what he was doing. Salomon's photographs showed that an ordinary person still existed behind the images power had officially approved.

Nearly a hundred years later, I hid a camera in Berlin too. But the models I photographed didn't have an ordinary person waiting behind the official face to be discovered. I wasn't finding a private moment — I was manufacturing a human being by photographing one.

One model in a black leather jacket leaned against the wall waiting for her cue. Weight on one leg, looking past the frame with a face that seemed to care about nothing in it. Reviewing the photo, I decided she was tired from the day. Maybe she hadn't slept well the night before, maybe she was faintly annoyed that a model who'd arrived after her had been called first. I never spoke to her. She never had a last night, or a this morning. But once a single tired posture existed, a whole day that would have produced it followed naturally behind it.

The fashion we remember always lives on the runway. But the time fashion is actually alive in runs behind that — after the show ends, still moving, still breathing, preparing the next story. We went backstage to meet RealSnap's model, Grace R. By the time the camera found her, she was already ready.

Between a pale sage wall and a dark doorway, she stood still in a sand-colored gown. The look Grace wears is built around a pale, sand-beige palette — warm, dry tones that recall dried leaves, folded paper, aged ivory carving, while radiating pleats repeat both the surface of a natural material and the trace of exacting craft.

The bodice is a strapless corset shape fitted to the body; folds that follow the curve of the chest gather sharply toward the center of the waist. Structured panels around the hip extend like a peplum, redesigning the body's real proportions into exaggerated form. Below, a midi-length skirt carries dense pleating and soft volume. Pointed-toe neutral pumps stay out of the dress's way, closing the silhouette long and quiet.

The first person to spot my fake credential wasn't security — it was the model wearing Look 28 from Prada Spring/Summer 2026. When I turned my pass face-down, she offered something of her own in return: she wasn't part of the official cast, but a standby, brought in in case someone dropped out. She'd been waiting since before dawn and had already been through fitting, but the original model showed up right before the show. In a few minutes she'd have to change back out of the clothes and leave, and a model who never actually walks the runway doesn't stay on the casting list.

Before I could answer, she tried to cover the lens with her palm and the flash went off anyway. Looking at the screen, she first told me to delete it, then changed her mind. She studied the half-obscured photo for a while and said, "Just send it to me. Today's the only proof I ever wore this." In exchange, she said she'd forget she'd ever clocked my pass. What was left was one awkward photograph, caught between an uninvited guest and an unofficial model.

Prada described this collection as built on the idea that clothing's meaning isn't fixed — it shifts depending on what it's placed next to. A red shirt and a black leather jacket read like a uniform; short bottoms and pointed shoes read like a sharp going-out look. The same woman who wore them was a model on the stairs and a guard in front of me, and once she let my fake pass slide, an accomplice after that. Maybe it's because she wore several roles at once in one night. She never made it onto the stage, yet of everyone I met that night, she was the one who understood the show's rules most precisely.

"The paparazzo came first for the already famous. This time, the order flipped."

Mia Y

In Federico Fellini's 1960 film La Dolce Vita, a photographer named Paparazzo appears, relentlessly trailing celebrities. That fictional figure is where today's actual job title — "paparazzi" — comes from. A name invented for someone who didn't exist came to describe a real way of shooting.

A paparazzo, strictly, chases someone already famous. But the order can run the other way too. Someone is photographed leaving a hotel unannounced, a tired expression or a mid-call moment ends up in a story, and we start to assume there's a life off-camera — a schedule, relationships, a day they'd rather not have shown. A paparazzi photograph proves a public figure has a private life, while simultaneously manufacturing the idea that this is a person whose private life is worth wanting to know.

The models who appear in this magazine had no morning before the camera arrived, no one on the other end of a call, no hotel to return to once the show ended. There was no privacy here that was actually violated. And yet one of them explained her own clothes, another waited to be called, someone handed over a business card, someone else covered the lens with her hand. The sense that something more exists behind a hidden face started, ironically, from that very palm held up to block it. The photographs didn't show us a private life — but they left the impression that one exists somewhere out of frame.

If Erich Salomon's candid camera found the ordinary human that already existed behind an official portrait, these photographs move in the opposite direction. The face comes first; the hours of waiting, refusing, and growing tired get attached afterward. Search any of these names now and the show they were backstage at, the look they wore, a short interview and a few sightings all come up together. The photographs can't prove these people existed. They just make it steadily harder to say they didn't.

A paparazzo, by definition, chases someone already famous. Tonight, the order was reversed. The photograph that could be chased came first — and only afterward did someone worth chasing appear behind it.
